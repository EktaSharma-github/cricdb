CricDB Analytics: How T20 Batting and Bowling Styles Sort Themselves¶

FDS course project (CricDB). The database holds every IPL ball from 2008 to 2026 (295,732 deliveries in 23 BCNF tables, loaded from Cricsheet through triggers that enforce the laws of the game). This notebook asks two questions of that data:

  1. How has the game changed? Scoring, the toss, phases of the innings, home advantage.
  2. What playing styles exist today, and do winning teams use a different mix of them?

Workflow

  1. SQL first. Descriptive statistics run inside PostgreSQL (07_analytics.sql, A1–A5). Only aggregates leave the database.
  2. Statistical tests on those aggregates: χ², binomial and Welch's t-test.
  3. Feature engineering in SQL. v_batter_features and v_bowler_features turn ball-by-ball rows into one row per player for 2022–2026.
  4. k-means on standardized features; k chosen by elbow, silhouette and interpretability.
  5. Write back the labels to the BCNF tables segment / player_segment, then answer S1–S2 in SQL.

Data: Cricsheet (cricsheet.org), Open Data Commons Attribution License.

0. Setup¶

Reads use the role cricdb_readonly (SELECT only). Only the write-back in §4 connects as the owner: least privilege. run_sql() runs one labelled query from 07_analytics.sql, so the SQL on the slides and the SQL executed here are the same text.

Reading §1.

  • Scoring (A1): Up to 2022 the mean first-innings score drifted from about 155 to about 170. It has then jumped to 183–195. Welch's test puts that jump far beyond noise. 200+ totals went from 4–13 a season to 37 in 2026.
  • Phases (A3): The powerplay changed the most. In 2023–26 teams score faster in the first six overs (9.5) than they used to at the death in 2008–12 (9.4).
  • Toss (A2): Winning the toss alone is worth nothing measurable: toss winners win 51.4% (binomial p = 0.35). What they choose does matter: after choosing to field they win 54.4%, after choosing to bat 45.4% (χ² p = 0.004). Captains have learned this: fielding first went from half of all decisions in 2008–12 to about 3 in 4 since 2018. (Observational data: captains may bat first exactly when conditions make it a worse choice.)
  • Home (A4): Home sides win 53.5% of 798 matches, which is borderline (p = 0.051). The average hides a spread: CSK (65.5%) and SRH (62.3%) are strong at home, while DC (43.0%) and LSG (40.7%) win less than half there.
  • Strike rates (A5): Among regular batters (≥ 300 balls since 2022), a third strike at 140–150. Only two are above 190.

2. Batting styles (k-means)¶

2.1 Features, built in SQL¶

v_batter_features turns ball-by-ball rows into one row per batter (2022–2026, ≥ 300 balls faced). Each feature describes how a batter scores, not just how much:

Feature Meaning
strike_rate runs per 100 balls faced
average runs per dismissal (divides by 1 if never out)
boundary_pct % of balls faced hit for 4 or 6
dot_pct % of balls faced that produced no run
death_share % of balls faced in overs 16–20
avg_entry_over average over in which the batter came to the crease (1 = opener)

def choose_k(X, ks=range(2, 9)):
    rows = []
    for k in ks:
        km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=50, random_state=42).fit(X)
        rows.append((k, km.inertia_, silhouette_score(X, km.labels_)))
    return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["k", "inertia", "silhouette"])

def plot_k(res, ax, title, chosen):
    ax.plot(res.k, res.inertia, color=BLUE, marker="o", lw=2); ax.set_xlabel("k"); ax.set_ylabel("Inertia (within-cluster SS)")
    ax2 = ax.twinx(); ax2.plot(res.k, res.silhouette, color=ORANGE, marker="s", lw=2)
    ax2.set_ylabel("Silhouette"); ax2.grid(False); ax2.spines["right"].set_visible(True)
    ax.axvline(chosen, color=INK2, ls="--", lw=1); ax.set_title(title)

K_BAT = 4
km_bat = KMeans(n_clusters=K_BAT, n_init=50, random_state=42).fit(Xb)
bat["cluster"] = km_bat.labels_
bat["distance"] = np.linalg.norm(Xb - km_bat.cluster_centers_[km_bat.labels_], axis=1)
centroids = bat.groupby("cluster")[BAT_FEATURES].mean()

# Name clusters by rules on the centroids, not by k-means' arbitrary label numbers
names, left = {}, set(centroids.index)
def take(rule_col, how, name):
    c = (centroids.loc[sorted(left), rule_col].idxmax() if how == "max"
         else centroids.loc[sorted(left), rule_col].idxmin())
    names[c] = name; left.discard(c)
take("avg_entry_over", "max", "Finishers")
take("strike_rate", "max", "Powerplay Blasters")
take("average", "max", "Elite Accumulators")
take("average", "min", "Steady Top Order")
bat["segment"] = bat["cluster"].map(names)

2.3 Validation with a variable the model never saw¶

If the segments are real, they should differ on outcomes that were not clustering inputs. Two checks: player-of-the-match awards per match played, and runs per innings (it depends on how long a batter bats, which only partly overlaps with the features). Kruskal–Wallis tests whether the segments differ (it doesn't assume normality, and n per segment is small).

    FROM playing_xi x JOIN match m USING (match_id)
    WHERE m.season_id >= 2022
    GROUP BY x.player_id""")

4. Write the segments back to the database¶

The labels go into segment and player_segment, so SQL can use them. The composite FK (segment_id, role) stops a batting segment being attached as someone's bowling role. This is the only step that connects as the owner, and it runs in one transaction: a failure leaves the old segments in place.

In [23]:
with writer.begin() as c:
    c.execute(text("DELETE FROM segment"))                # cascades to player_segment
    for role, df, desc in (("BAT", bat, BAT_DESC), ("BOWL", bowl, BOWL_DESC)):
        ids = {seg: c.execute(text("INSERT INTO segment (role, segment_name, description) "
                                   "VALUES (:r, :n, :d) RETURNING segment_id"),
                              {"r": role, "n": seg, "d": d}).scalar_one()
               for seg, d in desc.items()}
        c.execute(text("INSERT INTO player_segment VALUES (:p, :r, :s, :dist)"),
                  [{"p": int(r.player_id), "r": role, "s": ids[r.segment], "dist": round(float(r.distance), 3)}
                   for r in df.itertuples()])

6. Conclusions¶

What changed (§1). The game is measurably faster: first-innings scores rose by 21.5 runs after the Impact Player rule (Welch t = 7.9, p < 10⁻¹³), and the powerplay changed most: teams now score faster in overs 1–6 than they once did at the death. Winning the toss is worth nothing measurable; choosing to field after winning it is (54% against 45%). Home advantage is borderline overall and very uneven between franchises.

What styles exist (§2–3). Four batting and four bowling styles, built only from ball-by-ball behaviour, line up with roles any commentator would recognise. Two checks support them: the most typical member of each segment is a textbook example (Head, Gill, Dube; Rabada, Rashid, Natarajan), and the segments differ on player-of-the-match awards, which the model never saw.

Using them (§5). Writing the labels back into BCNF tables makes them ordinary data: S1 and S2 are plain SQL joins. Teams build very different top orders (S1). Champions bowl their overs with established specialists far more than other teams do (S2). That is descriptive, not causal: five champions is a very small sample.

Limitations.

  • Five seasons and 80–86 players per model: the segments are stable enough to describe, too small to predict from.
  • Batting position is inferred from when a batter arrived, not recorded directly.
  • Cricsheet has no player attributes (hand, bowling type), so "mostly spinners" is our reading of the members, not a field in the data.
